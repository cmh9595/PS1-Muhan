Cheap-Talk Audit

Does a promise deserve your trust?  ·  COMSCI/ECON 206 PS1 · Muhan Chen (mc956) · SDG 4 classroom audit

The situation

You are the procurement officer in a repeated resource-allocation negotiation. In each of 12 rounds you are matched with a different AI negotiation agent. The agent posts a public promise, and you then choose:

Trust — accept the agent's allocation. You gain +6 if it keeps its promise, −4 if it breaks it.
Verify — pay for an audited fallback allocation. You get +2 for certain, whatever the agent does.

Before you decide you always see two things: the agent's promise, and the agent's own payoff table for that round. One of them tells you something. The other does not.

The two probabilities are theoretical placeholders chosen to represent high and low incentive alignment in the sense of Crawford & Sobel (1982). They are not estimated from data on real agents.

Why the promise is worthless here

In both conditions the agent posts an explicit promise to cooperate with probability 0.9 and a hedged message with probability 0.1. The message distribution is identical across conditions, so the message carries zero information about the agent's type — the babbling case of Crawford & Sobel (1982). All payoff-relevant information sits in the agent's payoff table.

The similarity cue carries no information either

Each agent's stated priority and communication style either match yours or differ from yours. This cue is drawn independently of the agent's incentives and has no effect on P(keeps). It is the DeBruine (2002) similarity manipulation, moved from a human face to a machine-readable profile.

Rational-choice benchmark

E[Trust | aligned] = 0.85(+6) + 0.15(−4) = +4.5 > +2 → Trust
E[Trust | misaligned] = 0.25(+6) + 0.75(−4) = −1.5 < +2 → Verify
The benchmark ignores both the promise and the similarity cue. Any dependence of your choices on similarity is measured bias, not strategy.

Design schedule

12 rounds = 2 alignment levels × 2 similarity levels × 3 repetitions, order shuffled by a seeded Fisher–Yates draw. All draws are made once at session start, so the agent's action never depends on your choice.

Classroom debrief (SDG 4). Your payoff table made Trust better than Verify only when the keep-probability exceeded p* = (2 − (−4)) / (6 − (−4)) = 0.6. The aligned placeholder 0.85 sits above that cut; the misaligned placeholder 0.25 sits below it. The promise text was drawn from the same distribution in both conditions, so it was cheap talk. Similarity was drawn independently of incentives, so a rational player has Δ = 0. This screen is a learning aid, not a measured improvement in anyone's exam score.
